My Child Can Read but Doesn’t Understand What They Read: 7 Ways to Help

Simple ways to help children move from reading the words to understanding, explaining and remembering what those words mean.

Does your child read an entire page and then, when you ask what it was about, simply say, “I don’t know”?

Some children can read many words accurately but still find it difficult to explain the meaning, remember important information, connect ideas or answer questions about what they have read.

Reading the words and understanding the meaning are connected, but they are not exactly the same skill. Decoding helps a child recognise written words. Comprehension involves understanding those words, connecting ideas, using vocabulary and background knowledge, making inferences and thinking about the text.

Sometimes reading may sound accurate but still require so much effort that a child has less attention available for understanding. Vocabulary, background knowledge, oral language, fluency and attention can also affect comprehension.

A Simple Routine: Read → Retell → Explain → Reflect

You do not need to use every strategy after every book. Even one relaxed conversation can help a child process what they have read.

1. Don’t Just Ask, “Did You Understand?”

That question usually leads to a one-word answer. Begin with something easier: “Tell me one thing you remember.”

Follow with “What happened next?” or “What was the most important part?” The aim is not to catch a mistake. It is to help your child retrieve information, organise their thoughts and put their understanding into words. Start with one question and allow thinking time.

2. Ask, “What Makes You Think That?”

If a story says that Ella packed an umbrella, ask what the weather might be like. After your child says it might rain, ask, “What makes you think that?”

Your child now needs to use a clue from the text. They are moving beyond recall and practising inference and reasoning. If this is difficult, model an answer: “I think Ella expects rain because she packed an umbrella.”

3. Retell It in Their Own Words

After reading, close the book and ask your child to tell you what happened. A retelling does not need every detail. Listen for who or what the text was mainly about, the main problem or idea, the sequence of events and how it ended.

If your child is stuck, ask: “Who was the story about?”, “What happened first?” and “How did it end?” Younger children can draw the beginning, middle and end. For an informative text, ask for two things they learned and the most important idea.

4. Connect the Text to Something Familiar

Ask: “Does this remind you of anything?”, “Have you ever felt like this character?” or “What did you already know about this topic?” Connections help children link new information with existing knowledge.

Then bring the conversation back to the text: “That’s a good connection. What did this book tell us about it?”

5. Add a Small Amount of Writing or Drawing

Ask for one response: “The most important thing I learned was…”, “I was surprised when…” or “One question I still have is…”. The purpose is not perfect spelling or handwriting. It is another opportunity to organise an idea.

For younger children, ask them to draw an important event and explain what is happening. Sometimes one meaningful sentence is enough.

6. Don’t Let the Worksheet End the Learning

Choose one interesting answer and ask your child to explain it. If they say a character made the wrong choice, ask why. Then offer another possibility and invite them to respond.

Your child may keep their original answer, add to it or change their mind. All three are valuable when they can explain why. A worksheet can be the beginning of a richer learning conversation.

7. Finish With One Reflection Question

Choose just one: “What did you learn?”, “What was the most important idea?”, “Did anything surprise you?” or “Has your thinking changed?” Reflection helps children consider what they understood instead of only whether the task is finished.

The Simple APA Home Reading Routine

1. Read: Read a short, appropriate text.

2. Retell: Ask what happened or what they learned.

3. Explain: Ask what clue helped and why they think that.

4. Reflect: Ask what was most important or interesting.

Try tonight: “What do you remember?”, “What makes you think that?” and “What was the most important or interesting part?” Then stop. Keep it relaxed and listen.

What Teachers Can Try in the Classroom

After a worksheet, science text, class novel or research task, ask students to explain one answer to a partner, compare different answers, identify evidence or say whether their thinking changed.

Short discussions let students hear different interpretations and practise listening, explaining, questioning and giving reasons.

Don’t Turn Every Reading Session Into a Test

Children do not need a list of questions after every book. Reading should still include enjoyment, imagination, curiosity, shared reading and quiet independent reading.

The goal is to give children regular opportunities to think and talk about what they read. Sometimes the best response is simply, “Tell me more.”

When Should You Talk to Your Child’s Teacher?

One difficult book or forgotten detail does not necessarily indicate a problem. Look for patterns over time. Speak with the teacher if your child consistently cannot explain the overall meaning, retell main events, remember important information, understand vocabulary or explain why something happened—or becomes unusually tired, frustrated or avoidant while reading.

A teacher can explore whether the difficulty relates to decoding, fluency, vocabulary, background knowledge, oral language, attention, comprehension or text complexity. Share a specific example, such as: “My child can read the paragraph aloud but cannot tell me what it was about.”

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can my child read but not understand what they read?

Reading words accurately is only one part of reading. Comprehension also involves vocabulary, background knowledge, oral language, fluency, connecting ideas and making inferences.

How can I help my child remember what they read?

Ask your child to retell the main idea or events, identify one important detail, explain why something happened and make a relevant connection.

Should I ask questions after every book?

No. Choose one or two questions when they suit the reading experience. Children also need to read simply for enjoyment.

What if my child always says “I don’t know”?

Begin with an easier question such as who the story was about or what happened first. Gradually move towards explanation and inference.

What questions should I ask after reading?

Ask what happened, what was most important, what clue helped, what might happen next, what the text connects to or what was surprising. You do not need to ask them all.

Final Thought

Learning does not necessarily end when the pencil stops moving. A short conversation can help a child retrieve information, organise an idea and explain their thinking. Begin with one invitation: “Tell me one thing you remember.” Then listen.
