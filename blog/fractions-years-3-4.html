Fractions for Years 3–4

Fractions become much easier when children can see them, build them and talk about them before relying on symbols. This guide gives families a simple pathway from equal parts to fraction models, number lines, equivalent representations and fractions of quantities.

The key idea: a fraction describes equal parts of a whole or collection. Always identify the whole first, then ask how many equal parts it has been divided into.

What should children understand?

Equal parts

Children should recognise that the parts must be equal in size when a shape represents a fraction.

Unit fractions

Build understanding of one-half, one-third, one-quarter, one-fifth and one-tenth before moving to multiples.

Numerator & denominator

The denominator describes the number of equal parts in the whole; the numerator describes the parts being considered.

Different models

Use shapes, collections, fraction strips and number lines so children learn that the same fraction can look different.

A simple learning pathway

1. Start with real objects

Share fruit, paper strips, blocks or counters into equal groups. Ask, “What is the whole?” and “What fraction is one group?” This makes the language meaningful before introducing notation.

2. Move to pictures

Shade equal parts of rectangles, circles or collections. Encourage your child to explain both numbers in a fraction rather than memorising which is “top” and which is “bottom”.

3. Put fractions on a number line

Mark 0 and 1 first. Divide the interval into equal parts, then locate the fraction. This helps children see that fractions are numbers with positions and values.

4. Connect equivalent representations

Use fraction walls or diagrams to show when two fractions name the same amount. In Year 4, connect familiar fractions with decimal representations where appropriate.

Australian Curriculum connection

For Year 3, the Australian Curriculum focus includes representing unit fractions and their multiples in different models and combining fractions with the same denominator to complete a whole. The site's Year 3 fractions resources use AC9M3N02.

For Year 4, fraction learning extends into equivalent representations and connections between fractions and decimals, including AC9M4N03.

Try these five questions together

  1. A pizza is cut into 4 equal pieces. What fraction is one piece?
  2. A collection has 12 counters. What is one-third of the collection?
  3. Where would one-half go on a number line from 0 to 1?
  4. How could you prove that two fraction pictures represent the same amount?
  5. What is different about the numerator and denominator in 3/5?

Common mistakes to watch for

Unequal pieces

A shape split into unequal pieces does not automatically show equal fractions. Ask the child to check the size of every part.

Bigger denominator = bigger fraction

Compare the size of the parts. When the whole is fixed, dividing it into more equal parts makes each part smaller.

Forgetting the whole

“One-half” only makes sense once we know what the whole is. Ask, “Half of what?”

Keep practice short and conversational

Five minutes of asking a child to explain a fraction can be more useful than a long worksheet session. Ask them to draw it, place it on a number line, find it in an everyday situation and explain why their answer makes sense.

Parent prompt: “Show me another way to represent that fraction.” If your child can represent the same value in more than one way, you are building understanding rather than just answer-getting.
