Why Does My Child Keep Making the Same Spelling Mistakes?

And what the mistake may be telling you

Your child spells a word incorrectly.

You correct it.

A few days later, the same mistake appears again.

It is tempting to think, “We just went over this… why isn’t it sticking?”

But repeated spelling mistakes are often more useful than they first appear. They can give you a clue about what the child is noticing, what they are relying on, and what they have not yet connected.

Instead of asking only, “How do I get my child to remember this word?”, try asking:

“What is this spelling mistake telling me about how my child is thinking about the word?”

That small change can turn a frustrating correction into a short teaching moment.

Spelling Is More Than Memorising a List

Good spelling draws on several kinds of knowledge working together.

  • Phonology: noticing and working with the sounds in words.
  • Orthography: noticing letter patterns and spelling conventions.
  • Morphology: noticing meaningful word parts such as base words, prefixes and suffixes.
  • Etymology: understanding how the history or origin of some words helps explain their spelling.

Australian literacy guidance describes spelling as a complex system rather than simply a matter of memorising individual words. Students gradually learn how sound, spelling pattern, word structure and meaning work together.

What the Spelling Mistake Might Be Telling You

Look at the error before immediately giving the correct spelling.

What you notice What it may suggest What to investigate
sed for said The child may be relying strongly on sound. What does the word look like? Is there a useful word family or pattern?
runing for running The child may not yet be applying the doubling pattern. What happens when -ing is added?
jumpt for jumped The child may be hearing the ending but not connecting it with the spelling pattern. What does the base word jump become when -ed is added?
hoping written as hopping, or vice versa The child may be applying a pattern too broadly. What happens to the final e? Why does the spelling change in one word but not the other?
because written differently across attempts — for example, becaus or becouse The spelling may not yet be stable or automatic. Which part keeps changing? Sound, spelling pattern, vowel choice or memory?

The goal is not to diagnose a child from one spelling error. Instead, look for patterns across several examples.

Six Common Spelling Problems — and What to Do Next

1. “I can hear it, so I can spell it.”

Some children rely heavily on the sounds they hear.

That is an important spelling strategy — but English spelling cannot always be worked out from sound alone.

For example:

said → sed

The child has represented the sound they hear, but has not yet connected that sound with the conventional spelling.

Try this: ask, “What part of this word doesn't sound the way it looks?” Then compare the child's attempt with the correct spelling.

2. “I know the word, but I don't notice the pattern.”

A child may recognise a word but still not understand the spelling pattern that makes it work.

For example:

run → running

Rather than asking the child to memorise running as a completely new word, look at the relationship between the base word and the changed form.

Try this: write run → running and ask, “What changed? Why?”

This moves the child from memorising one word towards noticing a reusable spelling pattern.

3. “The ending sounds right, so I write what I hear.”

Word endings can cause repeated errors because the sound a child hears does not always give them the whole spelling.

For example:

  • jump → jumped
  • help → helpful
  • happy → happiness

These examples give children opportunities to notice that words are connected through meaningful parts.

Try this: cover the ending and ask, “What is the base word? What are we adding?”

4. “I learned the rule, but I am applying it everywhere.”

Sometimes the problem is not that a child has failed to learn a pattern.

They may actually be over-applying it.

For example, a child who has learned about doubling consonants may apply the pattern when it does not belong.

Try this: compare two or three words instead of correcting one word in isolation.

Ask:

  • What is the same?
  • What is different?
  • What pattern do you notice?
  • Does the pattern work in both words?

5. “I remember the word sometimes, but not every time.”

Inconsistent spelling does not automatically mean a child is careless.

A spelling may be known but not yet automatic, particularly when the child is also thinking about ideas, sentences, handwriting, punctuation and the content of a piece of writing.

Try this: practise a small number of useful words repeatedly in different contexts rather than giving a very long list.

6. “I know the spelling, but I don't know how the word is built.”

As children move through the primary years, they encounter increasingly complex vocabulary.

Knowing meaningful parts of words can help them make connections between related words.

For example:

  • help → helpful
  • happy → happiness
  • vision → television → revision

The Australian Curriculum's upper-primary English content includes knowledge of base words, prefixes, suffixes, word origins, letter patterns and spelling generalisations as part of developing spelling knowledge.

Try this: ask, “What other word do you know that is connected to this one?”

The APA Spelling Detective Method

Instead of simply correcting the word, use this short sequence:

1. SAY IT

Say the word naturally, then slowly if needed.

2. LOOK AT IT

Look at the child's spelling attempt. Don't immediately erase it.

3. FIND THE CLUE

Ask: Is the difficulty mainly about the sound, the spelling pattern, the word ending, or a meaningful word part?

4. COMPARE

Put the child's attempt beside the conventional spelling.

5. EXPLAIN

Ask the child to explain what they notice.

6. USE IT

Use the word in a new sentence or connect it to another related word.

The important step is Explain.

If the child can explain what changed and why, you have moved beyond simple copying towards actual word knowledge.

A 5-Minute Spelling Detective Activity

You do not need a 30-minute spelling lesson at home.

Choose three words your child has recently misspelled.

  1. Write the child's version.
  2. Write the correct version beside it.
  3. Circle the part that changed.
  4. Ask what the child notices.
  5. Connect the word to a related word or pattern.
  6. Use the word in a new sentence.

Child wrote: runing

Correct spelling: running

Ask: “What happened when we added -ing?”

Compare: run → running

Then try: hop → hopping

Now the child is not simply being told how to spell running. They are looking for a pattern they may be able to use again.

What If My Child Makes Lots of Different Spelling Mistakes?

Don't try to fix everything at once.

Choose a small group of words that show a similar pattern.

For example, if several errors involve word endings, investigate endings together. If several involve unfamiliar letter patterns, investigate the pattern rather than treating each word as a separate spelling list.

Think pattern, not pile.

Three related words can sometimes teach more than twenty unrelated words.

Should I Correct Every Spelling Mistake?

Not necessarily.

If your child is writing a story, journal entry or creative piece, stopping after every spelling error can interrupt the ideas they are trying to communicate.

Instead, choose a few high-value words to investigate.

You might say:

“Let's choose two words from your writing that we can become spelling detectives for.”

This keeps the focus on learning rather than turning every piece of writing into a correction exercise.

Why “Just Memorise It” Doesn't Always Work

Memorising some high-frequency or irregular words has a place in spelling instruction.

But children also need to understand how the spelling system works.

That is why a useful spelling conversation often sounds less like:

“Remember this word.”

and more like:

“What do you notice about how this word works?”

What Parents Can Do This Week

You only need five minutes.

Monday: Choose three words your child often misspells.

Tuesday: Look for the spelling pattern.

Wednesday: Connect one word to a related word.

Thursday: Use the words in new sentences.

Friday: Check whether your child can explain the pattern without help.

The goal is not perfect spelling in five days.

The goal is to help your child become better at noticing, explaining and applying how words work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my child keep making the same spelling mistake?

The repeated error may show that a particular part of the spelling system has not yet become secure. Look across several examples and ask what the child is relying on — sound, spelling pattern, word structure or memory.

Should I make my child write the correct word ten times?

Repeated writing can provide practice, but copying a word does not necessarily explain why it is spelled that way. Short, meaningful comparison and explanation can make the practice more useful.

Is sounding out words enough?

Sounding out is important, but it is only one part of spelling. Children also need knowledge of spelling patterns, word structure and, for some words, word history.

What if my child gets the spelling right one day and wrong the next?

Look at the wider pattern rather than judging one attempt. A child may know a spelling but not yet use it automatically, particularly when concentrating on other parts of writing.

When should I be concerned about spelling?

One or two repeated mistakes are not enough to draw conclusions. If spelling difficulties are persistent, widespread or significantly affecting your child's writing, talk with your child's classroom teacher or another appropriate literacy professional.

The Bigger Goal

Children do not need to become perfect spellers overnight.

They need to become better at noticing how words work.

When a repeated spelling mistake becomes a clue instead of a failure, the conversation changes.

Instead of:

“You got this wrong again.”

you can say:

“Interesting. Let's work out what this mistake is telling us.”

That is where spelling starts becoming something children can investigate — not just something they have to memorise.
