Year 6 Writing Activities: Narrative, Persuasive, Informative & Recount Writing

What should Year 6 students actually be practising when they sit down to write? It isn't just about filling a page with words — it's about making deliberate choices about what they write, who they're writing for, and how to make their ideas clear and effective.

A student who can write a fantastic story may still struggle to organise a persuasive argument. Another might be great at explaining facts but find it hard to make a recount sound interesting. Rather than practising one type of writing over and over, Year 6 students benefit from opportunities to write for different purposes and audiences.

What Should Year 6 Students Learn Through Writing?

Good writing practice works on both ideas and control.

The Australian Curriculum: English v9.0 describes Year 6 students as planning, creating, editing and publishing written and multimodal texts for imaginative, informative and persuasive purposes. It highlights paragraphs, complex sentences, expanded verb groups, topic-specific and vivid vocabulary, punctuation, spelling and visual features.

A strong Year 6 writing activity might ask a student to plan before writing, organise ideas into paragraphs, choose vocabulary for a particular audience, use different sentence structures, connect ideas so the writing flows, and edit and revise to make the meaning clearer.

In other words, the goal isn't simply "write more" — it's "make your writing work better."

1. Narrative Writing

Narrative writing gives students the chance to create a world, develop characters and take a reader somewhere. By Year 6, a narrative doesn't need to be a simple beginning-middle-end story — students can experiment with character motivation, setting, dialogue, tension, point of view and pacing.

Instead of telling the reader "Liam was scared," a student might show the feeling through action: "Liam stopped at the doorway. His hand tightened around the torch as another sound came from the dark hallway." The second example gives the reader something to imagine.

A Simple Year 6 Narrative Prompt

You arrive home and discover that one room in your house is completely different from the way you left it.

Ask students to think about: Who is the main character? What has changed? Why does it matter? What does the character notice first? What happens next, and how will the problem be resolved?

Challenge: try writing the same scene twice — once telling the reader what the character feels, and once showing it through actions, thoughts, dialogue and details. Compare the two. Which makes the reader feel more involved?

2. Persuasive Writing

Persuasive writing is about more than saying "I think this is a good idea" — a strong persuasive text gives the reader a genuine reason to care. Students can practise stating a clear position, developing reasons, supporting claims with examples or evidence, considering the audience, and responding to another point of view.

"I think schools should have more outdoor learning" is a starting point. A stronger argument explains why, who would benefit, and what evidence or examples support the position.

A Simple Year 6 Persuasive Prompt

Should primary school students have homework every night?

Before writing, ask students to create two columns — "For" and "Against" — then choose a position and build three strong reasons. For each reason, ask: what is my evidence or example? This prevents persuasive writing from becoming a list of unsupported opinions.

Challenge: write one paragraph arguing for a position, then try writing the strongest argument someone on the other side might make. This helps students understand persuasive writing is about responding to different viewpoints, not just stating an opinion.

3. Informative Writing

Informative writing has a different job — instead of entertaining the reader or convincing them of an opinion, the writer needs to explain a topic clearly and accurately. Students can practise selecting relevant information, organising it into logical sections, using topic-specific vocabulary and distinguishing important information from unnecessary details.

A Simple Year 6 Informative Prompt

How do Australian animals survive in their environments?

A student could choose one animal and explain where it lives, what it eats, how it protects itself, how it's adapted to its environment, and one interesting fact. The important question to keep asking is: what does my reader need to understand? — that shapes how they organise the information.

Challenge: write a short explanation of the same topic for two different audiences — a Year 2 student and a Year 6 student. The facts might stay similar, but vocabulary, sentence structure and level of detail should change.

4. Recount Writing

Recount writing is often treated as simply "write about what happened" — but a good recount is more than a list of events. Students need to organise events clearly and choose details that help the reader understand the experience, whether it's a school excursion, a sporting event or an unexpected moment.

A Simple Year 6 Recount Prompt

Write about a day when something did not go according to plan.

Instead of "We went to the zoo. We saw animals. We ate lunch. Then we went home," encourage students to think about what changed, which moment was most important, what they noticed, and how they felt. This gives the recount a purpose rather than turning it into a timetable of events.

Challenge: choose one ordinary event from the week and write about it as if it were an important story. What details would make someone else want to keep reading?

What Do These Four Types of Writing Have in Common?

Good writers use many of the same skills across all four genres.

  • Purpose: why am I writing this?
  • Audience: who will read it?
  • Structure: how should I organise my ideas?
  • Vocabulary: which words communicate my meaning best?
  • Sentences: can I vary my sentence structures?
  • Cohesion: do my ideas connect naturally?
  • Editing: what could I improve before I publish?

That last step is particularly important — good writing rarely appears perfectly on the first attempt.

Planning, Drafting and Editing: The Part Students Often Skip

Ask a child to write a paragraph and there's a good chance they'll start writing immediately — no plan, no pause, no checking. A short planning stage can make a huge difference.

1. Plan — write the main idea or purpose.
2. Organise — put related ideas together.
3. Draft — write the first version without trying to make every sentence perfect.
4. Revise — improve ideas, details, vocabulary and organisation.
5. Edit — check grammar, spelling and punctuation.
6. Publish — create the final version for the intended audience.

The Australian Curriculum v9.0 specifically includes planning, creating, editing and publishing as part of Year 6 writing — so editing shouldn't be treated as something students do only when they've made mistakes. Editing is part of writing.

Common Year 6 Writing Misconceptions

A few habits worth catching early, across all four genres.

"I need lots of big words to make my writing sound better." Not necessarily — the best vocabulary communicates the idea accurately and suits the purpose and audience.

"Every sentence should be long." A mixture of sentence lengths makes writing clearer and more interesting.

"Persuasive writing is just saying what I think." A strong argument needs reasons, examples and support, not just an opinion.

"My first draft is finished." A first draft is a starting point — good writers reread, revise and edit.

"Narrative writing doesn't need planning." Even a short plan helps a narrative stay focused with a clear sequence of events.

"More detail always makes writing better." Not if the detail is irrelevant — the goal is useful detail, not simply more words.

A 10-Minute Year 6 Writing Practice Routine

You don't need an hour-long writing lesson every night.

Minutes 1–2, Plan: what is the purpose? Who is the audience? What are the main ideas?

Minutes 3–7, Write: focus on getting the ideas down — don't stop every few seconds to correct spelling.

Minutes 8–10, Improve: choose just three things to check — one sentence to improve, one word to replace, one punctuation or spelling check.

Small, regular practice can be much less overwhelming than asking a student to produce a huge piece of writing every time.

Easy Year 6 Writing Prompts

  • Narrative: You find a note hidden inside an old book. It has your name on it.
  • Persuasive: Should students be allowed to choose their own homework?
  • Informative: Explain how a chosen Australian animal survives in its environment.
  • Recount: Write about a day when something unexpected happened.

Challenge: choose one topic — like "school excursions" — and write about it in two different ways. Write one persuasive paragraph arguing that excursions are important, then write a recount about a favourite excursion. Same broad topic, completely different purpose — that's the flexibility good writers develop.

How Parents and Teachers Can Help

You don't need to correct every sentence a child writes — sometimes, asking a good question is more useful.

Instead of "This sentence is wrong," try: "Can you read that sentence aloud? Does it sound the way you want it to?" Instead of rewriting a paragraph for them, ask:

  • What are you trying to explain here?
  • What does your reader need to know?
  • Could you give an example?
  • Is this the strongest word you could use?
  • Could these two ideas be connected?
  • What could you remove, or explain more clearly?

The goal is to help students become independent editors of their own writing.

Australian Curriculum Connection

Year 6 writing in the Australian Curriculum v9.0 focuses on creating written and multimodal texts for imaginative, informative and persuasive purposes. Content description AC9E6LY06 specifically includes planning, creating, editing and publishing, along with paragraphs, complex sentences, expanded verb groups, tense, topic-specific and vivid vocabulary, punctuation, spelling and visual features.

That's why Year 6 writing practice should go beyond simply asking students to "write a paragraph" — students need opportunities to plan, create, revise, edit and write for different purposes and audiences.

Frequently Asked Questions

What types of writing should Year 6 students practise?

Year 6 students can benefit from practising imaginative, informative and persuasive writing, as well as useful text forms such as recounts. The important goal is to develop the ability to write for different purposes and audiences.

What should a Year 6 student be able to do in writing?

Year 6 students should be developing their ability to plan, create, edit and publish texts. They should also be working on paragraph organisation, sentence variety, vocabulary, spelling, punctuation and clear communication of ideas.

How can I help my Year 6 child improve their writing?

Start with short, regular practice. Give your child a clear purpose and audience, allow time for planning, and encourage them to reread and improve their own work rather than correcting everything for them.

How often should Year 6 students practise writing?

Regular short practice can be useful. A focused 10-15 minute writing activity several times a week can give students opportunities to practise without making writing feel overwhelming.

What is the difference between informative and persuasive writing?

Informative writing aims to explain or communicate information clearly. Persuasive writing aims to influence the reader's thinking or position by presenting a claim supported by reasons, examples or evidence.

How can students make narrative writing more interesting?

Encourage students to develop characters, settings, problems and events rather than simply listing what happened. Dialogue, sensory details, varied sentence structures and showing emotions through actions can also make narratives more engaging.

Final Thought

Year 6 writing isn't about producing the longest piece of writing possible — it's about learning to make better choices. Better ideas. Better organisation. Better vocabulary. Better sentences. Better explanations. And, perhaps most importantly, better decisions about what the reader actually needs.

Give students different reasons to write, different audiences to write for, and enough time to improve what they've written. The goal isn't simply to help a child write more — it's to help them become a more confident, thoughtful and purposeful writer.
