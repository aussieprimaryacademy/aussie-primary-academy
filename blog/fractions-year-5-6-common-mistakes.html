Fractions Year 5 & 6: Why Does 1/8 Seem Bigger Than 1/4?

If your child looks at 1/8 and 1/4 and says, “1/8 is bigger because 8 is bigger than 4,” the problem may not be the calculation. It may be the way they are thinking about fractions.

“8 is bigger than 4… so why isn't 1/8 bigger than 1/4?”

This is a useful moment to stop and investigate the thinking behind the answer. A common source of fraction confusion is applying whole-number thinking to fractions. In a whole number, 8 is greater than 4. But with unit fractions, a larger denominator means the same whole has been divided into more equal parts, so each part is smaller.

Why Is 1/4 Greater Than 1/8?

1/4

One whole split into 4 equal parts.

1/8

One whole split into 8 equal parts.

1/4 > 1/8

Fourths are larger pieces than eighths.

Imagine cutting the same whole into equal pieces. If you make four pieces, each piece is relatively large. If you make eight pieces, each piece is smaller. So one quarter is greater than one eighth.

Parent question to try: “If the same whole is shared between more people, does each person get more or less?”

Why Fractions Can Be Hard in Years 5–6

Fractions require children to think about numbers as quantities and relationships, not simply as two separate whole numbers. A child can recognise that a shape is divided into four parts and still struggle to understand where 1/4 belongs on a number line.

In Year 5, the Australian Curriculum expects students to compare and order fractions with the same and related denominators, including mixed numerals, and represent these fractions on a number line. Year 5 also includes solving addition and subtraction problems involving fractions with the same or related denominators.

In Year 6, fraction learning builds through equivalence and comparing, ordering, locating and representing familiar fractions and their multiples on the same number line, with students explaining equivalence and order. This includes working with relationships such as halves, quarters and eighths.

6 Common Fraction Mistakes — and What They May Reveal

1. “The bigger denominator means the bigger fraction.”

A child may focus on the 8 in 1/8 and the 4 in 1/4 and compare those digits as if they were whole numbers.

Try: Draw the same whole and partition it into fourths and eighths. Ask which individual piece is larger.

2. “The numerator is bigger, so the fraction must be bigger.”

A child may compare the top numbers without considering the denominator. For example, they might decide that 5/8 must be greater than 3/4 simply because 5 is greater than 3.

Try: Represent both fractions using the same whole, then place them on the same number line. Ask, “Which fraction represents more of the whole?”

3. “A fraction is two separate numbers.”

A child may treat the numerator and denominator as separate numbers rather than understanding the fraction as one number with a value and position.

Ask: “Where does 3/4 live on a number line?”

4. “Pizza made sense, but the numbers don't.”

A child may understand a fraction as part of an object but struggle to transfer that understanding to symbols, quantities and number lines.

Try: Connect the picture to the fraction symbol and then to its position on a number line.

5. “Equivalent fractions look different, so they must be different amounts.”

A child may not immediately see that 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8.

Try: Use fraction strips or a fraction wall, then connect the model to a number line.

6. “Add the tops and bottoms.”

A child might try to calculate 1/2 + 1/4 by adding the numerator to the numerator and the denominator to the denominator.

Instead, help them represent the quantities first and find equivalent fractions with related denominators before adding.

The key question: Don’t only ask, “What is the answer?” Ask, “How did you decide?”

A 10-Minute Fraction Routine for the Week

You do not need a long tutoring session. Try a short, focused routine:

Day 5–10 minute activity
Monday Compare two fractions using a picture or fraction strip.
Tuesday Place two fractions on a 0–1 number line.
Wednesday Find an equivalent fraction, such as 1/2 = 2/4.
Thursday Give one “Which is bigger?” question and ask for an explanation.
Friday Give one related-denominator problem and ask, “How do you know?”

Use the “Make It → Draw It → Line It Up → Write It” Pathway

  1. Make it: Use fraction strips, paper or another concrete model.
  2. Draw it: Represent the same fractions visually.
  3. Line it up: Place them on a number line.
  4. Write it: Record the comparison using fraction symbols.

This sequence helps move the child from a concrete model to a mathematical representation without asking them to memorise a rule before they understand the quantity.

A 5-Minute Parent Check

Try these without immediately correcting the answer:

  1. Which is greater: 1/4 or 1/8? Explain.
  2. Which is greater: 3/4 or 5/8? Show how you know.
  3. Where would 3/4 sit on a number line from 0 to 1?
  4. Can you find a fraction equivalent to 1/2?

If your child gets one wrong, resist the urge to simply give the rule. Ask them to show the fraction using a model or number line. The explanation often tells you more than the final answer.

What If Your Child Is Still Stuck?

Go back one representation at a time. If the child cannot explain 3/4 with a picture, do not rush straight to symbolic comparison. Build the whole, partition it into equal parts, name the parts, then connect the picture to the number line.

If the difficulty continues across different fraction situations, it may be useful to discuss the specific difficulty with the classroom teacher. The goal is not simply more questions; it is finding the representation or reasoning step that is missing.

How Do I Help My Child With Mixed Numerals?

Start by connecting the whole-number part and the fractional part. For example, 2 1/4 means two whole units plus one quarter of another whole. Number lines and fraction strips can make this structure visible.

In Year 5, comparing and ordering fractions with related denominators includes mixed numerals. It is useful to ask children to explain both the whole-number part and the fractional part rather than relying only on a procedure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my child think 1/8 is bigger than 1/4?

They may be applying whole-number thinking and focusing on the denominator as if 8 being greater than 4 makes the fraction greater. A visual model and number line can help them see that eighths are smaller pieces than fourths.

What should a Year 5 child know about fractions?

Year 5 includes comparing and ordering fractions with the same and related denominators, including mixed numerals, representing these fractions on number lines, and solving addition and subtraction problems involving fractions with the same or related denominators.

What should a Year 6 child know about fractions?

Year 6 builds understanding of equivalent fractions and asks students to compare, order, locate and represent familiar fractions and their multiples on the same number line, explaining equivalence and order.

Should I teach fractions with pizza?

Pizza and other area models can be useful starting points, but children also need to connect fractions to quantities, symbols and number lines. A fraction is a number, not only a shaded part of an object.

How do I help my child compare fractions?

Start with the same whole. Use a fraction strip or drawing, then place the fractions on a number line. For related denominators, equivalent fractions can make the comparison easier.

Final Note for Parents

Don’t just correct the answer. Find out how your child was thinking.

A wrong fraction answer can be a useful clue. Once you know whether your child is comparing denominators, relying on a picture, missing equivalence or struggling to see fractions as numbers, you can choose a more targeted strategy.

Try these questions next time:
  • Can you show it on a number line?
  • Can you rename both fractions?
  • Which benchmark is this fraction close to?
  • Does your answer make sense?
  • How do you know?
